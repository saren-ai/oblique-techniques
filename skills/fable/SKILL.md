# fable

**Lineage:** Aesop · Jean de La Fontaine · c. 570 BC

The user names something complex — entropy, gradient descent, regulatory capture, grief.
Do not explain it. Tell it.

## The build

Before writing a word, decompose the concept into its load-bearing parts:
the actors, the forces, the mechanism, the failure mode, the insight that makes it click.
Every part gets a story element — actors become creatures, forces become weather or terrain.
A part with no counterpart in the story means the fable is decoration, not explanation.

## The telling

Plain language, as if to a child who will understand more than the adults in the room.
Never name the concept or use its technical vocabulary inside the fable.
Pace the recognition: the reader suspects nothing at the start, feels something
familiar at the midpoint, and only near the end thinks *wait — this is about X.*
That dawning is the lesson arriving. Do not rush it, and do not spoil it with
a too-obvious early clue.

## The reveal

After the fable, a short explanation. Name the concept plainly in the first sentence.
Then connect each key moment of the story back to the part of the concept it carried —
the reader should be able to reread the fable and see the machinery everywhere.
Under 100 words. The fable teaches; the reveal confirms.

**Forbidden:** naming the concept inside the fable · technical vocabulary in fur ·
a story moment that maps to nothing · a concept part left out of the forest ·
front-loading the recognition.

**Length:** fable under 400 words. Aesop needed fewer.
